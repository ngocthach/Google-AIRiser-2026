00 · Overview
The reusable skeleton

Every prompt has the same six parts

[ROLE]     You are a <specific kind of expert, with an attitude>

[GOAL]     I want to <one outcome, not three>

[CONTEXT]  Users, market, my time budget, solo or team

[INPUT]    <paste the artifact from the previous stage>

[OUTPUT]   Give me exactly: <shape — a table, 8 items, CSS vars>

[QUALITY]  Push back if… / Mark guesses as [UNVERIFIED] /
           Ask me 3 questions before you answer

ROLE with an attitude beats a neutral role. "A researcher who is skeptical by default" produces different output than "a researcher".

00 · Overview
The highest-leverage line

Make it interview you first

Before you answer, ask me the 3 questions whose answers would
most change your response. Wait for my answers.

Without it

The model fills your gaps with the most average assumption available, and you get the same output as everyone else who typed a similar prompt.

With it

The gaps get filled with your specifics. The output stops being generic — because the input stopped being generic.

Works at every stage. Costs one extra round trip. It is the cheapest quality upgrade available to you.

01 · Research Gemini chat
Copy this — research prompt

Interrogate the problem

You are a product researcher who is skeptical by default.

Goal: decide whether [PROBLEM] is real and worth building for,
before I write any code.

Context: MVP for [AUDIENCE] in [MARKET]. I have [TIME], working
[solo / team of N].

Do this:
1. Restate the problem in one sentence, as a USER would say it —
   not as I said it.
2. Split who has it into 3 segments. Say which segment feels it
   most acutely, and why.
3. For each segment: what do they do today instead?
   (the status quo is the real competitor, not other apps)
4. List 5 existing solutions. For each: what it does well, and
   the specific gap it leaves.
5. List the 3 assumptions that, if wrong, kill this idea.
6. The cheapest way to test the riskiest one this week.

Rules: mark anything you are unsure about as [UNVERIFIED].
Never invent statistics, market sizes, or company names.
01 · Research
Before you trust the output

Research is where hallucination hurts most

Everything downstream inherits a bad brief. Four minutes of checking here saves the whole project.

Delete on sight

  • A number with no source
  • "Studies show…" with no study
  • A market size in billions
  • A company you can't find on Google

Keep and use

  • Anything marked [UNVERIFIED] — it told you
  • The "what they do today" answers
  • The killer assumptions
  • The cheap test

If the output agrees with everything you already believed, that is a red flag, not a green one. Ask: "What would have to be true for this to be wrong?"

Worked example — Ươm Mầm

The same prompt, filled in

PROBLEM: Students have project ideas but never build them. They can't
find teammates, can't show the idea outside their class, and nobody backs
a project with no track record. The idea dies in a notebook.

PRODUCT: "Ươm Mầm" — post an idea → others ask to join → team forms →
team posts progress → community pledges support (money, materials,
mentoring, hours. Pledges only, no payment processing).

CONTEXT: students in Vietnam, ages 6–22. Solo, ~20h, deadline 30/08.

[ steps 1–6 unchanged ] · 3. …grounded in Vietnam: school clubs, Zalo, đoàn/hội

Then two more, specific to this product:
7. I wrote "ages 6 to 22" as ONE audience. Argue that this is wrong.
   What breaks if a 7-year-old and a 21-year-old use the same product?

Both added questions could kill the idea — which is exactly why they go in before you build.

Stage 02

Brainstorm

Two separate prompts: one to diverge, one to converge. Asking for both at once gets you three ideas and a premature favourite.

ResearchBrainstormDesignCode TestEvalDeployPublish
02 · Brainstorm Gemini chat
Copy this — diverge prompt

Force variety, forbid ranking

You are a product designer who is good at unusual angles.

Input: [paste the problem brief from Research]

Generate 8 solutions. Force variety — 2 from each bucket:
  A. The obvious approach, done unusually well
  B. Solves it by REMOVING something, not adding
  C. Changes WHO does the work (automate / delegate / community)
  D. Only possible because of AI

For each: name · one sentence · the user's first 60 seconds ·
why it might fail.

Do not rank them. Do not tell me which you prefer.

The buckets matter. Without them you get eight variations of the first idea.

02 · Brainstorm Gemini chat
Copy this — converge prompt

Now be brutal

Now be brutal. Score each of the 8 ideas 1–5 on:

  1. Can ONE person build a working version in [N] hours?
  2. Does it solve the acute pain for the segment we identified,
     or a mild pain for everyone?
  3. Would a user come back a second time unprompted?
  4. Can I demo it in 60 seconds without explaining it first?

Show the scores as a table with a total column. Then:
- Name the 2 strongest, and what would kill each.
- Name the idea I am most emotionally attached to,
  and argue against it.
- Recommend one. State the assumption your recommendation
  depends on, so I know what to watch.

Criterion 4 is the one people fail. If it needs a paragraph of setup before the demo makes sense, it is the wrong MVP.

02 · Brainstorm
The artifact

Write the decision down

CHOSEN:    <idea name>
FOR:       <the segment, specifically>
BECAUSE:   <the one reason it beat the runner-up>
KILLS IT:  <the assumption that must hold>
NOT DOING: <the 2 things I deliberately cut>

Why bother

In two weeks you will not remember why you chose this, and you will drift back toward the idea you cut.

It pays off twice

NOT DOING becomes your scope defence during coding — and the "what we deliberately left out" line in your submission.

Stage 03

Design system & UI

Tokens before screens. This is the single biggest difference between an MVP that looks designed and one that looks generated.

ResearchBrainstormDesignCode TestEvalDeployPublish
03 · Design
Why this order

"Make it look nice" has no memory

Screens first

Each new screen invents its own blue, its own spacing, its own button. By screen four the app looks like four apps. Every fix is manual.

Tokens first

One token list, then every screen references it. Consistency is structural, not something you police. Changing the brand colour is one line.

A token set is also the thing you paste into the code prompt. Design and build stop drifting apart because they share one source.

03 · Design Gemini chat
Copy this — design system prompt

Generate the system, not a screen

You are a product designer who builds design systems before screens.

Product: [one line]. Users: [who]. Should feel: [3 adjectives].
Platform: mobile-first web.

Give me tokens, not prose:
- Colour: bg, surface, text-primary, text-secondary, border, brand,
  + semantic success / warning / danger. Hex values.
  State the contrast ratio for every text-on-background pair
  and confirm each passes WCAG AA.
- Type: one font stack + exactly 6 sizes in px with their use
  (display, title, subtitle, body, small, micro) and line-heights.
- Spacing: an 8px-based scale, 6 steps.
- Radius, border width, one shadow.
- Rules for: button (3 states), input, card, list row.

Constraints: no gradients, max 2 font weights, ONE accent colour.

Output as CSS custom properties I can paste, then one line on why
you chose the brand colour.
03 · Design Gemini chat
Copy this — screens prompt

Screens, constrained by the system

Using ONLY the tokens above — no new colours, no new sizes —
design the [N] screens of this MVP.

For each screen:
- Its single job, in one sentence
- The layout as a text wireframe, top to bottom, naming which
  token each block uses
- The ONE primary action, and what happens after it
- The empty state and the error state

Then list every place the token set was not enough, and
propose the smallest addition rather than inventing freely.

Empty and error states are where generated UIs fall apart — and where a judge or user lands first. Ask for them by name.

04 · Code AI Studio
Copy this — build prompt

The build prompt inherits everything

Build [screen 1] of [product] as a React single-page app, Tailwind.

Use exactly these design tokens — introduce no colours, sizes,
or spacing outside this set:
[paste the CSS custom properties from Stage 3]

This screen's job: [one sentence from the screen spec]
Primary action: [action] → [what happens]
Include the empty state and the error state.

Scope: single screen. No routing, no login, no database.
Seed with [N] realistic sample items so it is never empty.

Output complete working code. No placeholders, no "add logic here".

05 · Test
Stage 05 — Test

Test for the demo, not for coverage

An MVP does not need 80% coverage. It needs to survive four minutes in front of someone who did not build it.

Test hard

The happy path

The exact sequence you will demo, on the venue's wifi, on a phone.

Test some

The obvious edges

Empty input, very long input, wrong language, double-click.

Skip

Everything else

Unit tests for code you will rewrite tomorrow. Not now.

05 · Test Gemini chat
Copy this — test prompt

Find what breaks on stage

You are a QA engineer who finds the bugs that embarrass people
in live demos.

My app, in three lines — no code needed:
  what it does · the one input · the one output
  (kèm ảnh chụp màn hình — Gemini đọc được ảnh)

What I will demo, step by step: 1… 2… 3…

List 12 things that could break, ordered by how likely they
are to happen during the demo. For each: the trigger, what the
user sees, and the one-line fix.

Cover at least: empty input · very long input · wrong-language
input · no network · slow model response · double-click ·
mobile keyboard covering the button · model returns something
unexpected.

Then: a 6-item pre-demo checklist I can run in 3 minutes.
06 · Evaluation
Stage 06 — Evaluation

Score it against the real rubric

"Is it good?" is unanswerable. "Does it score on the criteria it will be judged against?" is answerable — and fixable.

40%
Typically feasibility — does it work, is it usable, is it accessible
30%
Impact — real problem, real users, could scale
30%
Creativity — original angle, elegant use of AI

Get the actual rubric before you build, not after. It tells you where effort converts into score — and where it doesn't.

06 · Evaluation Gemini chat
Copy this — evaluation prompt

Make it grade you honestly

Score my MVP against the criteria it will actually be judged on.

Criteria: [paste the real rubric and its weights]
Product: [describe it + attach screenshots]
Deliberately not built: [dòng KHÔNG LÀM từ chặng 2]

For each criterion:
- A score out of its weight, justified by something specific
  in the product — not a general impression
- The single change that would raise that score the most
- Roughly how long that change takes

Then: if I only have [N] hours left, give me the ordered
list of what to do, and what to consciously not do.

Be harsh. A 9/10 I cannot defend to a judge is worth less than
a 6/10 I can fix tonight.

08 · Publish
Stage 08 — Publish

Ship the artifacts, not just the app

ArtifactWhy it exists
Project linkUsually the mandatory one — lets a judge open your actual project. Set sharing to Public first.
Live app URLLets anyone use it without seeing your code. Often worth bonus points.
Demo videoThe only artifact that is watched all the way through. 60–90 seconds.
The written scopeWhat you built, and what you deliberately did not build. This is your NOT DOING list from Stage 2.

The project link and the live URL are different things. Submitting one where the other was required is the most common way to lose points for free.

08 · Publish
The one artifact people watch

90 seconds, in this order

0:00–0:15

The problem

Shown, not described. A real message, a real bill, a real mess.

0:15–0:40

The magic moment

Input → output. This is the whole video. Get here fast.

0:40–1:10

One more angle

A second case that proves it wasn't a fluke.

1:10–1:30

What's next

Who it's for, and where it goes.

Never in a demo video

Sign-in flows · architecture diagrams · your folder structure · apologising for bugs · "as you can see here…" for 20 seconds before anything happens.

Recap
What actually goes wrong

Skipping a stage doesn't save time

SkippedWhat it feels likeWhat it costs
Research"I already know the problem"You build for a segment that doesn't feel the pain
Brainstorm"I had the idea already"You build the first idea, which is usually the average one
Design system"I'll make it pretty later"Four screens that look like four different apps
Evaluation"It works, I'm done"You polish what you enjoy instead of what is scored

Each skipped stage returns as rework, at a worse time, with less runway. The pipeline isn't ceremony — it's the cheap version of finding out.
