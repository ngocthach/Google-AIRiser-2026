00 · Overview
The reusable skeleton

Every prompt has the same six parts

[ROLE]     You are a <specific kind of expert, with an attitude>

[GOAL]     I want to <one outcome, not three>

[CONTEXT]  Users, market, my time budget, solo or team

[INPUT]    <paste the artifact from the previous stage>

[OUTPUT]   Give me exactly: <shape — a table, 8 items, CSS vars>

[QUALITY]  Push back if… / Mark guesses as [UNVERIFIED] /
           Ask me 3 questions before you answer

ROLE with an attitude beats a neutral role. "A researcher who is skeptical by default" produces different output than "a researcher".

00 · Overview
The highest-leverage line

Make it interview you first

Before you answer, ask me the 3 questions whose answers would
most change your response. Wait for my answers.

Without it

The model fills your gaps with the most average assumption available, and you get the same output as everyone else who typed a similar prompt.

With it

The gaps get filled with your specifics. The output stops being generic — because the input stopped being generic.

Works at every stage. Costs one extra round trip. It is the cheapest quality upgrade available to you.

01 · Research Gemini chat
Copy this — research prompt

Interrogate the problem

You are a product researcher who is skeptical by default.

Goal: decide whether [PROBLEM] is real and worth building for,
before I write any code.

Context: MVP for [AUDIENCE] in [MARKET]. I have [TIME], working
[solo / team of N].

Do this:
1. Restate the problem in one sentence, as a USER would say it —
   not as I said it.
2. Split who has it into 3 segments. Say which segment feels it
   most acutely, and why.
3. For each segment: what do they do today instead?
   (the status quo is the real competitor, not other apps)
4. List 5 existing solutions. For each: what it does well, and
   the specific gap it leaves.
5. List the 3 assumptions that, if wrong, kill this idea.
6. The cheapest way to test the riskiest one this week.

Rules: mark anything you are unsure about as [UNVERIFIED].
Never invent statistics, market sizes, or company names.

Open up. Then be brutal.

PROMPT 1 — DIVERGE. Run this alone.
Input: [the problem brief]. Generate 8 solutions.
Force variety — 2 from each bucket:
  A. the obvious approach, done unusually well
  B. solves it by REMOVING something, not adding
  C. changes WHO does the work    D. only possible because of AI

PROMPT 2 — CONVERGE. Only after reading all 8.

Criterion 4 is where most ideas die. If it needs a paragraph of setup before the demo makes sense, it is the wrong MVP. A machine falling apart while a child taps frantically needs none.

02 · Brainstorm
The artifact

Write the decision down

CHOSEN:    Spark — a machine falls apart, and the English words are the
           controls that fix it
FOR:       children 8–11 who read independently, and the parent who sets
           them up
BECAUSE:   a child replays a game they lost. No child rereads a lesson.
KILLS IT:  a slow reader losing every round in eight seconds — the timer
           turns reading speed into the difficulty
NOT DOING: leaderboards, audio, several children per account, offline

Why bother

In two weeks you will not remember why you chose this, and you will drift back toward the idea you cut.

It pays off twice

NOT DOING becomes your scope defence during coding — and the "what we deliberately left out" line in your submission.

04 · Design
Why this order

"Make it look nice" has no memory

Screens first

Each new screen invents its own blue, its own spacing, its own button. By screen three the app looks like three apps. Every fix is manual.

Tokens first

One token list, then every screen references it. Consistency is structural, not something you police. Changing the brand colour is one line.

A token set is also the thing you paste into the code prompt. Design and build stop drifting apart because they share one source.

04 · Design
The trap in the previous slide

Tokens buy consistency. They do not buy taste.

What "for children" gets you by default

Rainbow primaries. Everything rounded until nothing has a corner. A mascot nobody asked for. Confetti on every correct answer. Consistent, accessible, and identical to every other kids' app generated this year — because the model reached for the average.

What makes it yours

  • An atmosphere you committed to, in one sentence
  • ONE accent, with a reason, used only where I can act
  • A geometry decision — and one you did not take
  • A density decision — for this user, in this posture
  • A list of the defaults you forbade

Tokens are the memory. The principles are the taste. The next prompt asks for both — most people only ask for the first half.

04 · Design
What changes when the user is eight

Four numbers you would otherwise get wrong

Touch

56px minimum, not 44

44px is an adult finger on a phone at a desk. This is a child on a tablet, on the floor, with three seconds on the clock.

Reading

18px labels, readable at a glance

The label is not prose here — it is a button the child must recognise in under a second. Small type is not a style choice, it is a difficulty setting.

Reading level

A token, not a note

--reading-max-words:40 and --reading-cefr:"A2". Stage 05 passes these straight into the model call — so they live in the token set, or they get lost.

Failure

Two kinds, and they differ

Losing a round is the fun part — make it funny, restart in one tap. Getting a word wrong is never commented on: no red, no WRONG, no report card.

The fourth card is the one people get wrong. A game needs a fail state; a learner does not need to be told they failed. Decide that once, in the system, or every screen will invent its own punishment.

05 · Code
Before you go looking for pictures

The book is not your asset library

The illustrations that gave you the idea are copyrighted. So is most of what an image search returns for "old telephone".

Best

Generate it

AI Studio's build agent makes images on demand. Ask for the machine and its six parts in the same prompt that builds the round, and they arrive in one house style.

Fine

Public domain

Wikimedia Commons, museum open-access collections. Check the licence per image, not per website.

Never

Photograph the book

A submission that ships someone else's artwork is not a submission.

This is the kind of thing that disqualifies you after you have won — which is considerably worse than not winning.

A children's app on an open database

05 · Code AI Studio
Copy this — layer 4

The AI layer, and the mistake everyone makes

Generate the machines. Before the round starts — never during it.

When a machine is opened for the first time, call the model once:
- 6 parts, each a noun a child can see and point at, at --reading-cefr
- for each part, 3 fault states as "THE <PART> IS <STATE>!",
  at most --reading-max-words words each
- 3 sentences on why this invention mattered, for the win screen

Write all of it into machines/{id} and serve every later play from
Firestore. A round is 60 seconds of real time — a model call is not.

When the child beats a machine 3 times, regenerate the part names one
level harder (PIPE → PRESSURE PIPE) and store that as a second set.
Never mix two levels inside one round.

You cannot call a model mid-round

This is the caching lesson with the excuse removed. A three-second timer and a two-second API call are incompatible. Generate up front, store, serve.

The game gets harder in English, not in speed

--reading-cefr was written in Stage 04 and is read by a prompt in Stage 05. The model raises the vocabulary as the child improves — a database cannot do that, and it is your answer on Creativity.

06 · Test
Stage 06 — Test

Test for the demo, not for coverage

An MVP does not need 80% coverage. It needs to survive four minutes in front of someone who did not build it.

Test hard

The happy path

The exact sequence you will demo, on the venue's wifi, on the device you will actually hold up.

Test some

The obvious edges

Two fingers at once, a tap a fraction after the timer, slow generation, no network, losing five rounds in a row.

Skip

Everything else

Unit tests for code you will rewrite tomorrow. Not now.

This product has two audiences. A judge watches the demo; a child uses the app. The child finds things the judge never will — so test as the child, then demo for the judge.

07 · Evaluation
Stage 07 — Evaluation

Score it against the real rubric

"Is it good?" is unanswerable. "Does it score on the criteria it will be judged against?" is answerable — and fixable.

40%
Feasibility — for a children's game, accessibility is feasibility: 56px targets, 18px labels, a fault you can read in greyscale, a round playable on a keyboard. You already built it. Claim it.

30%
